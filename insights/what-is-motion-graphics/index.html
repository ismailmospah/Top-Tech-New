What is motion graphics, and when do you need it?

Motion graphics is design that has been given time: type, shapes, illustration and data moving in sequence to explain something. It is not a replacement for filmed video, nor a cheaper version of it — it is a tool that does things a camera cannot, and fails at things a camera does better.

A practical definition

Motion graphics is graphic design with the dimension of time added. Instead of presenting information in a still image, you present it as a sequence: this first, then this, then the result.

The fundamental difference from filming is that the thing does not have to exist. You can show how a service works, how money moves between two accounts, or how a figure grows across three years — none of which can be pointed at with a camera.

That is why motion dominates in categories that sell ideas and systems rather than physical objects: software, fintech, services, logistics, insurance, education.

When motion is the right choice

  1. When the explanation is complex

    A product that takes three paragraphs to describe usually takes fifteen seconds to show. Visual sequence explains what prose stumbles over.

  2. When there is nothing to film

    A service, a piece of software, an internal process. Filming these usually ends in generic office footage that says nothing.

  3. When you need volume and consistency

    A motion system with reusable templates produces dozens of on-brand pieces. Filming offers no equivalent economy.

  4. When the first second decides everything

    In a feed, movement in the opening moment is what stops the thumb. Motion controls that moment with complete precision.

  5. When you are presenting numbers

    Animated data is understood far faster than a table or a paragraph. A figure counting up in front of the eye lands harder than one written down.

  6. When budget is limited and the need is continuous

    Not because it is always cheap, but because its cost can be controlled precisely through the level of detail — something a shoot day does not allow.

When motion is the wrong choice

We say this despite motion being where this studio started: there are cases where it is simply wrong.

  • When the product is physical and bought by eye. Clothing, food, furniture, jewellery — people want to see the thing itself.
  • When you need human trust. A real customer's face and voice cannot be replaced by animation.
  • When the place is the product. A restaurant, a hotel, a clinic, a property development.
  • When you are selling craft. Seeing the making is the message.

In most of these cases the answer is not choosing between the two but combining them: real footage with animated type and graphics carrying the explanation and the numbers.

The most used formats

  • Explainer video. Explains a product or service in a minute or less. The most requested.
  • Reels and social video. Short, vertical, fast-paced, built for the feed rather than cropped into it.
  • Paid ads. Multiple versions of one idea to test which stops the scroll.
  • Product launches. Announcing a new feature or service in a single visual language.
  • Brand motion. How the logo moves, how type enters, how scenes transition — the system that makes every video feel like the same brand.
  • Data presentation. Reports, results and statistics made comprehensible in motion.

What separates good motion from bad

Not the number of effects. The most effective videos are usually the visually simplest.

  • One idea. A video trying to say five things is remembered for none.
  • Script before animation. Movement does not rescue weak writing; it makes it more irritating.
  • Pacing that serves comprehension. Too fast prevents reading, too slow loses attention.
  • Audio equal to picture. Bad sound closes a video in two seconds regardless of how it looks.
  • Designed for silent viewing. Most views start muted — if the video is not understood without sound, it has lost most of its audience.

Questions

Frequently asked

What is the difference between motion graphics and animation?

Animation is the broader term, covering every kind of moving image including characters and storytelling. Motion graphics specifically animates design elements — type, shapes, icons, data — and its purpose is explanation rather than narrative.

Does motion work for a small brand?

Yes, and usually better than a large production would. A defined motion system with templates lets a small brand publish consistent video every week, which achieves far more over a year than one expensive film published once.

How long does a motion video take?

The decisive stage is not animation but approval of the script and style frames. Once those are settled the rest is predictable, and the cost of changes rises sharply once animation begins — more on that in the video production cost article.

Do we need a visual identity before motion?

Not strictly, but its absence shows. Without a defined system for type, colour and movement, every video will look different from the last. That is why we sometimes start with the identity system before production — every video afterwards becomes faster, cheaper and more consistent.
